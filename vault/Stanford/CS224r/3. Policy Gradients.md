
## Summary

Policy gradients are the foundation of online RL. Rather than imitating a demonstrator, we directly optimize the expected reward by computing gradients of the policy objective. The core insight is simple: **make actions that led to good outcomes more likely, and actions that led to bad outcomes less likely** — a formalization of trial-and-error learning.

This note covers:
- How to derive the gradient of the RL objective
- The REINFORCE algorithm
- Why the gradient is noisy and how to fix it (causality + baselines)
- How to implement policy gradients efficiently
- Extending to off-policy learning via importance sampling and KL constraints

## 1. From Imitation Learning to RL

**Overview:** Imitation learning optimizes behavior by copying a demonstrator. Policy gradients break free from this constraint by optimizing directly for reward.

| | Imitation Learning | Policy Gradients |
|---|---|---|
| Data source | Expert demonstrations $\mathcal{D}$ | Policy's own rollouts |
| Objective | Match demonstrator | Maximize expected reward |
| Can surpass demonstrator? | No | Yes |
| Learns from practice? | No | Yes |

The imitation learning objective was:
$$\min_\theta -\mathbb{E}_{(s,a) \sim \mathcal{D}}[\log \pi_\theta(a | s)]$$

The RL objective we want to maximize instead:
$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\left[\sum_t r(s_t, a_t)\right]$$

**Online RL loop:**
1. Initialize policy (randomly, via imitation learning, or heuristics)
2. Run policy to collect a batch of trajectories
3. Improve policy using the batch
4. Repeat

## 2. Deriving the Policy Gradient

**Overview:** We want $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$, but we can't compute it analytically because it requires integrating over all trajectories. A key algebraic trick lets us turn this into a computable expectation.

**Step 1 — Express $J(\theta)$ as an integral:**

$$J(\theta) = \int p_\theta(\tau) \, r(\tau) \, d\tau$$

**Step 2 — Take the gradient:**

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \int \nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau) \, r(\tau) \, d\tau$$

**Step 3 — Apply the log-derivative trick:**

$$\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau) = p_\theta(\tau) \, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)$$

This lets us rewrite the gradient as an expectation:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\left[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) \cdot r(\tau)\right]$$

**Step 4 — Expand $\log p_\theta(\tau)$:**

The probability of a trajectory factors as:
$$p_\theta(\tau) = p(s_1) \prod_{t=1}^T \pi_\theta(a_t | s_t) \, p(s_{t+1} | s_t, a_t)$$

Taking the log turns products into sums:
$$\log p_\theta(\tau) = \underbrace{\log p(s_1)}_{\text{no }\theta} + \sum_{t=1}^T \log \pi_\theta(a_t | s_t) + \underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^T \log p(s_{t+1} | s_t, a_t)}_{\text{no }\theta}$$

Only the middle term depends on $\theta$, so:
$$\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) = \sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t | s_t)$$

**Final gradient:**

$$\boxed{\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t | s_t)\right)\left(\sum_{t=1}^T r(s_t, a_t)\right)\right]}$$

**Intuition:** This looks almost identical to the imitation learning gradient — but instead of treating all actions equally, we weight them by the total trajectory reward. Actions taken in high-reward trajectories get reinforced; actions taken in low-reward trajectories get suppressed.

## 3. REINFORCE Algorithm

**Overview:** The expectation above can be estimated from samples. This gives us the REINFORCE algorithm — the simplest online RL algorithm.

**Monte Carlo estimate of the gradient:**

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left(\sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_{i,t} | s_{i,t})\right)\left(\sum_{t=1}^T r(s_{i,t}, a_{i,t})\right)$$

**Algorithm:**
1. Sample $N$ trajectories $\{\tau^i\}$ by running current policy $\pi_\theta$
2. Compute the gradient estimate above
3. Update: $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \nabla_\theta J(\theta)$
4. Repeat

**Key property: on-policy.** We must collect fresh data after each update because the gradient estimate assumes samples come from the *current* $\pi_\theta$. This makes data collection expensive.

## 4. Why Policy Gradient is Noisy

**Overview:** Even with many samples, the policy gradient estimate has high variance, which makes learning slow and unstable.

**Example — learning to walk:**

Suppose we run 5 rollouts and observe:
- $\tau_1$: falls backwards (negative reward)
- $\tau_2$: falls forwards (slightly positive)
- $\tau_3$: falls forwards more (slightly positive)
- $\tau_4$: takes one small step, then falls (medium positive)
- $\tau_5$: takes one large step backwards, then small step forward

The gradient encourages "falling forwards" because it has positive reward — even though falling is clearly not the goal. The issue is **reward scale sensitivity**: the gradient can't distinguish between "a little good" and "very good."

**Two main problems:**
1. **Reward scale sensitivity** — gradient magnitude depends on the absolute reward values, not relative quality
2. **Credit assignment** — actions at time $t$ receive credit for rewards at all times, even $t' < t$ (before the action was taken)

---

## 5. Reducing Variance: Causality

**Overview:** An action at time $t$ cannot affect rewards that happened *before* $t$. Using the full trajectory reward to credit each action is wasteful and adds noise.

**Fix:** Replace total trajectory reward with the **reward-to-go** (sum of future rewards from time $t$ onward):

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_{i,t} | s_{i,t}) \underbrace{\left(\sum_{t'=t}^T r(s_{i,t'}, a_{i,t'})\right)}_{\hat{Q}_{i,t} \text{ (reward-to-go)}}$$

**Why this helps:** Past rewards are just noise when evaluating an action. Dropping them reduces variance without changing what we're optimizing (the expectation is unchanged — this is unbiased).

---

## 6. Reducing Variance: Baselines

**Overview:** Even with causality, all trajectories with positive reward will be reinforced — even mediocre ones. A baseline shifts reward so that only *above-average* behavior gets reinforced.

**The problem illustrated:**

Suppose we're learning to fold a jacket:
- $\tau_1$: doesn't touch the jacket (reward: 0)
- $\tau_2$: folds only the sleeves (reward: 0.5)
- $\tau_3$: flattens but doesn't fold (reward: 0.5)
- $\tau_4$: folds the jacket neatly (reward: 1)

Without a baseline, the gradient reinforces all of these — the policy learns to sometimes do mediocre things.

**Fix:** Subtract a constant baseline $b$ from the reward:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\left[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) \cdot (r(\tau) - b)\right]$$

**Does this change the gradient?** No — subtracting a constant baseline does **not** change the gradient in expectation (it is **unbiased**):

$$\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) \cdot b\right] = b \cdot \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}\left[1\right] = 0$$

But it **reduces variance** by centering the reward signal. The simplest choice is the average reward:

$$b = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r(\tau^i)$$

**Full gradient with causality + baseline:**

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_{i,t} | s_{i,t}) \left(\sum_{t'=t}^T r(s_{i,t'}, a_{i,t'}) - b\right)$$

**Summary of variance reduction tricks:**

| Trick | What it does | Biased? |
|---|---|---|
| Causality | Use reward-to-go, not full trajectory reward | No |
| Baseline | Subtract average reward | No |

> Even with these tricks, policy gradient is best used with **dense rewards** and **large batch sizes**.

## 7. Implementation via Surrogate Objective

**Overview:** Computing $N \times T$ separate backward passes to get the gradient is expensive. Automatic differentiation gives us a smarter way.

**The problem:** Naively computing the gradient requires a backward pass for each (trajectory, timestep) pair — $O(N \cdot T)$ passes total.

**The fix:** Define a **surrogate objective** whose gradient equals the policy gradient. Then differentiate through it in one backward pass:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{surrogate}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^T \log \pi_\theta(a_{i,t} | s_{i,t}) \cdot \hat{Q}_{i,t}$$

where $\hat{Q}_{i,t} = \sum_{t'=t}^T r(s_{i,t'}, a_{i,t'}) - b$ is treated as a **constant** (stop gradient).

Computing $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{surrogate}}$ gives exactly the policy gradient we derived — in a single backward pass.

## 8. Off-Policy Policy Gradients

**Overview:** On-policy learning requires collecting new data after every gradient update, which is expensive. Off-policy methods let us reuse data from a previous policy — but require corrections to account for the distributional shift.

**Definitions:**
- **On-policy:** gradient updates use only data from the *current* policy $\pi_\theta$
- **Off-policy:** gradient updates can reuse data from *previous* policies

### Importance Sampling

**Core idea:** If we have samples from an old policy $\pi_{\theta'}$ but want to estimate an expectation under $\pi_\theta$, we can reweight the samples:

$$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}\left[\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} f(x)\right]$$

Applying this to policy gradients, if we want to update policy $\pi_\theta$ using data from $\pi_{\theta'}$:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\theta'}(\tau)}\left[\frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_{\theta'}(\tau)} \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) \cdot r(\tau)\right]$$

Expanding the trajectory probability ratio:

$$\frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_{\theta'}(\tau)} = \prod_{t=1}^T \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t | s_t)}{\pi_{\theta'}(a_t | s_t)}$$

**Problem with the product form:** This product over $T$ timesteps can become extremely large or extremely small, leading to very high variance.

**Practical approximation — per-timestep expectation:**

Instead of taking the expectation over full trajectories, we approximate by taking the expectation over individual timesteps. This avoids the exploding product:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta') \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{\pi_\theta(a_{i,t} | s_{i,t})}{\pi_{\theta'}(a_{i,t} | s_{i,t})} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_{i,t} | s_{i,t}) \left(\sum_{t'=t}^T r(s_{i,t'}, a_{i,t'}) - b\right)$$

**Off-policy algorithm:**
1. Collect batch of data using current policy $\pi_{\theta'}$
2. Apply **multiple** gradient updates using importance-weighted gradient
3. Update $\theta'$ to the new $\theta$
4. Repeat

This is more data-efficient: one data collection step, multiple gradient steps.

### KL Constraint

**The problem:** If we apply many gradient updates before collecting new data, the updated policy $\pi_\theta$ can drift far from the data-collection policy $\pi_{\theta'}$. When this happens, the importance weights become inaccurate and the gradient estimate degrades.

**Fix:** Constrain how much the policy is allowed to change per update using a **KL divergence** penalty:

$$\max_\theta \; J_{\theta'}(\theta) \quad \text{subject to} \quad D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\theta'}) \leq \epsilon$$

where $J_{\theta'}(\theta)$ is the importance-weighted objective and $\epsilon$ is a small threshold.

**Why KL divergence?** It measures how different two distributions are. Keeping $D_{\text{KL}}$ small ensures the new policy $\pi_\theta$ stays close to $\pi_{\theta'}$, so the importance weights remain reliable.

**Key tradeoff:** A tighter KL constraint means safer updates but slower learning. A looser constraint allows more progress per batch but risks degraded gradient estimates.

> This KL-constrained off-policy policy gradient is the conceptual basis for **PPO** (Proximal Policy Optimization), which we'll see in the actor-critic lecture.
